Case study 02

Developer Portal Redesign

A static design file had quietly become the team's primary tool for aligning clients and partners on payment flows. It was failing in three directions at once — and the product was about to grow in a way that would make all three worse.

Role
UX Researcher & Designer
Methods
Stakeholder interviews · Thematic synthesis · Iterative prototyping
Scale
7 stakeholder interviews · 5 prototype iterations
Output
Research synthesis and a working single-page portal prototype

The problem

The product team maintained a design-file "flow worksheet" — a static visual reference showing how payment passkey enrollment and authentication looked on mobile. It was the team's primary artifact for aligning clients, partners, and internal regional teams on the experience.

Three problems compounded each other:

  • It was mobile-only. Teams had no visual reference for web, embedded, or responsive layouts — a routine client question with no quick answer.
  • It was expensive to maintain. Every update was a significant manual effort, so the worksheet drifted out of sync with the live product.
  • It couldn't be shared directly. Sharing constraints meant teams exported flows into slide decks, which stripped the interactivity and added a delay to every request.

The product was also about to scale — new regions, new locales, new authentication technologies. The format was not going to survive that growth.

My role

  • Owned this end to end — research, synthesis, and build — as both researcher and designer
  • Scoped and ran the stakeholder interview series, choosing participants to represent both the teams that produced the worksheet and the teams that consumed it downstream
  • Synthesized the interviews into six themes, validating each against the full interview set before presenting it
  • Designed and built all five prototype iterations in HTML, CSS, and JavaScript as a single self-contained file
  • Tied every prototype feature back to a specific finding, so the artifact argued the case rather than illustrating it
  • Presented to product leadership and handed the prototype off as the engineering blueprint

Research process

I ran a stakeholder interview series across the teams that relied on the worksheet most: client services, regional partnership teams, and product leadership. Seven interviews, chosen to represent both the teams that produced the worksheet and the teams that consumed it downstream.

The goal was to understand how it was actually being used, rather than how it was intended to be used — those turned out to be different things. I synthesized findings thematically and validated each theme against the full interview set before presenting it.

What I found

01

The worksheet was a source asset, not a tool

It was rarely used directly. Teams extracted screens, dropped them into decks, and re-contextualized them for every client conversation. Functionally it was a screen library — but a static, hard-to-navigate one that nobody treated as the destination.

Strong consensus
02

Visual gaps became communication failures

When clients asked about browser behavior, embedded layouts, or how a flow would look at a different screen size, teams had no visual answer. They fell back on verbal description or raw, unpolished captures — in a conversation where the whole point was to show rather than tell.

Strong consensus
03

Maintenance friction was the structural problem

Every update was described as a project unto itself. That created a persistent lag between what the product did and what the worksheet showed, which meant teams and clients were routinely working from flows that were quietly out of date. This was the root cause the other findings kept pointing back to.

Strong consensus
04

Clients couldn't picture the flow in their own product

A recurring problem: clients couldn't visualize how the flows would sit inside their own interface. Decontextualized screens don't answer "what does this look like in my app?" — and some regional teams had started building their own ad hoc mockups to fill the gap, which is a reliable signal that a central artifact isn't doing its job.

Mixed consensus
05

Scale was about to outpace the format

With expansion into new regions, locales, and authentication technologies, the worksheet would need to cover substantially more surface area than it already struggled to maintain. The problem was going to get worse on a known schedule.

Strong consensus
06

It was an internal alignment problem, not just a client one

The worksheet sat inside a large, distributed collaboration environment, with regional teams building parallel materials to serve their own needs. Framing it purely as a client-facing asset understated what it was actually being asked to do.

Mixed consensus

From findings to prototype

Rather than hand over a slide deck of recommendations, I built the argument. Five iterations of an interactive prototype, each grounded in specific findings — so every feature could be traced back to something a stakeholder had actually said.

  • V2 Baseline Established the core concept — a hosted, filterable flow viewer with direct navigation, replacing extraction with browsing. Addresses finding 01.
  • V3 Filters and multi-device Use-case, language, and currency filters, plus device switching across mobile, tablet, and desktop — closing the responsive gap and building for multi-locale scale from the start. Addresses findings 02 and 05.
  • V4 Documentation and environments A developer documentation panel — sequence diagrams, guides, API reference — docked alongside the viewer, plus environment switching. One fewer separate source to keep in sync, and earlier visibility for engineering. Addresses findings 03 and 06.
  • V5 Export, design intent, and design-file integration One-click export for regional teams; a toggle surfacing stakeholder vision quotes inline as annotations; and an integration allowing real design frames to replace coded mockups — solving the maintenance problem at its source rather than adding another process to maintain. Addresses findings 01 and 03.

What the final prototype did

A fully self-contained single HTML file with no dependencies, covering five payment integration types:

  • Use-case, language, currency, and integration-type filtering — six languages, seven currencies
  • Multi-device preview across mobile, tablet, and desktop
  • Environment switching and a feature-toggle panel for beta methods
  • Design-file integration mapping real frames into flow slots
  • Export to document, vector, presentation, image, and video formats
  • Integrated developer documentation per flow
  • Full-flow overview and step-by-step demo views
  • Design intent annotations surfacing stakeholder quotes as tooltips

Outcome

The prototype was used to align product leadership on the redesign direction. It became the primary artifact in stakeholder reviews and was handed off as the blueprint for engineering build-out.

It replaced the competing parallel decks that regional teams had been maintaining separately, and demonstrated that the maintenance problem could be solved at the source rather than by layering on another update process.

The finding underneath the findings. Partway through synthesis it became clear the worksheet was being asked to serve two audiences with incompatible needs. Internal teams wanted version history and export. Clients wanted a filtered, contextual demo. The original format tried to be one thing and served neither well — and no amount of maintenance was going to fix that. The prototype architecture separates the two rather than compromising between them.
