Case study 01

Payment Authentication Setup Concept Testing

Three rounds of unmoderated qualitative testing on a payment authentication setup experience. Iterations focused on CTA button copy and presenting enough context for users without overburdening them with information.

Role
Lead UX Researcher
Methods
Unmoderated concept testing · A/B qual · AI-assisted transcript analysis
Scale
n = 27 · 3 rounds
  • R1 n = 12 · US + UK
  • R2 n = 10 · UK
  • R3 n = 5 · UK
Output
Figjam deliverable with key takeaways covering primary research questions, charts, and video clips

The study

The product and design team was testing copy variants of a passkey-based payment authentication setup flow — a checkout experience where a user enrolls in a passkey service (utilizing face ID, fingerprint, or PIN/pattern) to replace one-time passcodes on future purchases.

Four questions were asked of every variant, each round:

  • Why are participants likely or unlikely to enroll in passkey authentication?
  • Did they understand what they were signing up for?
  • Did the authentication experience meet their expectations?
  • Where did participants hesitate; where did they have questions or concerns?

The two-purchase structure

Each participant completed two tasks: 1) purchasing an item as a first-time user, which prompts the enrollment flow, then 2) purchasing a second item at a different merchant, testing the authentication they had previously set up.

One of the primary goals of the research was to determine whether the perceived value gained from authentication was worth the initial enrollment effort.

My role

  • Designed flexible study structure: initial A/B qualitative testing, followed by two additional rounds to evaluate design iterations based on findings (process was similar to RITE testing)
  • Wrote discussion guide, established comparison metrics, set up unmoderated tests for UK and US participants, and managed screening/recruitment and data quality on UserTesting platform
  • Cleaned transcripts using Claude Code and used AI-assisted analysis to identify key insights and patterns across the full transcript set
  • Presented recommendations that informed CTA copy, messaging content, and surfacing primary value proposition
  • Created Figjam deliverables for each round’s report and carried findings into the next round’s iteration on enrollment experience

Method

Transcript conventions

All sessions were recorded and transcribed, then cleaned against a documented convention: filler words removed but never inside quotations; corrections notated with the original preserved; ambiguous inferences flagged rather than resolved silently; speech crossing task boundaries marked. Common speech-to-text errors were logged and corrected systematically — the transcription reliably mangled the domain vocabulary in the same ways, so the fixes could be applied consistently.

The convention was written first and applied with Claude Code — the filler and notation passes, and the recurring domain-vocabulary corrections. Having the rules fixed in advance is what let the work be delegated: the tool applied a documented convention rather than exercising judgment about what a participant meant.

Cleaned transcripts were treated as the source of truth and frozen once finalized.

AI-assisted analysis

Coding was AI-assisted, using Claude Code: candidate takeaways and the passages supporting them were surfaced across the full transcript set rather than by hand, against the cleaned transcripts rather than the raw ones. What went into each round’s report was selected and written from there.

A/B qualitative design

In rounds 1 and 2, two distinct participant groups each experienced one variant. This allowed direct comparison of comprehension, willingness, and mental model formation between variants — rather than only measuring change before and after within a single group. Round 3 dropped the split and tested one variant, since the question by then was whether the value proposition landed, not which of two did.

What each round tested

Each round carried the previous round’s result into the next stimulus, so the comparison narrowed as it went — from what to call the step, to how much to say about it, to whether the value landed.

  • R1 Terminology — Continue vs Passkey Two flow variants side by side: the standard framing already in use, against one reframed around the passkey concept itself. This was the only round fielded in both markets: US participants proved markedly less familiar with payment authentication methods and did not see the value in enrolling, so rounds 2 and 3 ran in the UK only.
  • R2 Context density — reduced vs full Both variants now used the Continue framing, moving the comparison to how much explanation to attach at the point of the ask: a reduced-context variant against a full-context one. This is where enough had to be defined rather than assumed.
  • R3 Value proposition A single variant rather than a comparison. With the wording and the amount of context settled, the final round checked whether the value proposition was surfaced clearly enough for participants to recognize and understand what they were being offered.

Outcome

The study directly informed:

  • The messaging hierarchy on the enrollment prompt screen — what to lead with, and how much supporting context to carry alongside it
  • Terminology standardization across the product, resolving inconsistent usage between related but distinct concepts
