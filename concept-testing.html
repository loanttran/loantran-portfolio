Payment Authentication Setup Concept Testing

June 2026

Three rounds of unmoderated qualitative testing on a payment authentication setup experience. Iterations focused on CTA button copy and presenting enough context for users without overburdening them with information.

Role
Lead UX Researcher
Methods
Unmoderated concept testing · A/B qual · AI-assisted transcript analysis
Scale
n = 27 · 3 rounds
  • R1 n = 12 · US + UK
  • R2 n = 10 · UK
  • R3 n = 5 · UK
Output
Figjam deliverable with key takeaways covering primary research questions, charts, and video clips

The study

The product and design team was testing copy variants of a passkey-based payment authentication setup flow — a checkout experience where a user enrolls in a passkey service (utilizing face ID, fingerprint, or PIN/pattern) to replace one-time passcodes on future purchases.

Research objectives:

  • Why are participants likely or unlikely to enroll in passkey authentication?
  • Did they understand what they were signing up for?
  • Did the authentication experience meet their expectations?
  • Where did participants hesitate; where did they have questions or concerns?

The two-purchase structure

Each participant completed two tasks: 1) purchasing an item as a first-time user, which prompts the enrollment flow, then 2) purchasing a second item at a different merchant, testing the authentication they had previously set up.

One of the primary goals of the research was to determine whether the perceived value gained from authentication was worth the initial enrollment effort.

My role

  • Designed flexible study structure: initial A/B qualitative testing, followed by two additional rounds to evaluate design iterations based on findings (process was similar to RITE testing)
  • Wrote discussion guide, established comparison metrics, set up unmoderated tests for UK and US participants, and managed screening/recruitment and data quality on UserTesting platform
  • Cleaned transcripts using Claude Code and used AI-assisted analysis to identify key insights and patterns across the full transcript set
  • Presented recommendations that informed CTA copy, messaging content, and surfacing primary value proposition
  • Created Figjam deliverables for each round’s report and carried findings into the next round’s iteration on enrollment experience

Method

AI-assisted analysis

First, each unmoderated session was reviewed and an initial pass of key findings was composed — this was to ensure that any AI-assisted analysis could be evaluated for output quality to prevent instances of overgeneralized statements.

Participant takeaways were generated using Claude Code and the quotes supporting them were surfaced across the full transcript set. Each round’s deliverable was written from AI-assisted insights, supporting evidence, and guided by my initial findings.

A/B qualitative design

In the first two rounds, two distinct participant groups each experienced one variant. This allowed direct comparison of comprehension, willingness, and expectations between separate experiences using a mix of qualitative questioning and metrics based on Likert, 7-point scale. The final round tested a single variant, bringing together preferred design elements and messaging.

What each round tested

Each round carried the previous round’s result into the next stimuli/iterations, so the comparison and focus sharpened as the rounds progressed.

  • R1 CTA Copy — Continue vs Passkey Two enrollment flow variants side by side: general “Continue” terminology vs. passkey-specific wording. This was the only round fielded in both markets: US participants proved markedly less familiar with payment authentication methods and did not see the value in enrolling, so later rounds ran in the UK only.
  • R2 Context Density — Reduced vs Full Both variants now used the Continue/general framing, moving the focus to how much explanation to attach at the point of the ask: a reduced-context variant against a fuller one. This is where enough had to be defined — primarily, what benefits equated to value for participants.
  • R3 Value Proposition A single variant rather than a comparison. With the copy and the amount of context settled, the final round checked whether the value proposition was surfaced clearly enough for participants to recognize and understand what they were being offered.

Outcome

The study directly informed:

  • The messaging hierarchy on the enrollment prompt screen — what to lead with, and how much supporting context to present to users moving quickly through checkout
  • Correction of participants' misassumption of a key value prop that led to abandonment of enrollment screen
  • Understanding of where specific terminology can help or hinder participant comprehension and enrollment likelihood

Conducted at a global payment network. Company and product names, participant details, and proprietary metrics have been omitted.
