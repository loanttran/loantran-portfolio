Case study 01

Payment Authentication Setup Concept Testing

Three rounds of unmoderated qualitative testing on a payment authentication setup experience. Iterations focused on CTA button copy and presenting enough context for users without overburdening them with information.

Role
Lead UX Researcher
Methods
Unmoderated concept testing · A/B qual · AI-assisted transcript analysis · Participant-count verification
Scale
n = 27 · 3 rounds · ~9 participants per round · UK and US · Remote
Output
Round reports with six verified takeaways each

The study

The product team was testing a passkey-based payment verification flow — a checkout experience where a cardholder enrolls a biometric to replace one-time passcodes on future purchases. Two framings of the same flow were tested side by side: one using standard enrollment language, one reframed around the passkey concept itself.

Three questions were asked of every variant, every round:

  • Why are participants likely or unlikely to set up verification?
  • Did the authentication experience meet their expectations?
  • Where did they hesitate, and where did they have questions or concerns?

The two-purchase structure

Each participant completed two tasks: buy a toaster as a first-time user, which triggers the enrollment prompt — then buy a bicycle at a different merchant, which tests whether the enrolled passkey works seamlessly and whether they notice.

The second purchase was the point. Watching the passkey work automatically at a different merchant was the moment most participants understood what they had signed up for — which is why it was built into the protocol rather than left to a follow-up question.

My role

  • Designed the three-round study, including the A/B qualitative structure and the two-purchase task sequence that made the post-enrollment experience observable rather than hypothetical
  • Developed the participant-count verification method and applied it to every candidate takeaway across all three rounds
  • Wrote the transcript cleaning convention — including the systematic corrections for speech-to-text errors that recurred in the domain vocabulary — and directed its application with AI assistance
  • Wrote the discussion guide and set up the unmoderated test across UK and US participants
  • Authored each round’s report — six verified takeaways per round — and carried findings into the next round’s stimulus
  • Presented recommendations that informed enrollment prompt placement, messaging hierarchy, and terminology standardization across the product

Method

Participant-count verification

Every candidate takeaway was checked against the full participant set before it could be elevated to a key finding. Each was tagged three ways:

  • Supports — participants with clear, explicit evidence
  • Partially supports — participants with indirect or weaker evidence
  • Does not support — participants who contradicted it

This exists to prevent impressionistic synthesis. A finding that sounds compelling but rests on one or two vivid participants does not get presented as a pattern — and the vivid sessions are exactly the ones that stay with you.

Coding was AI-assisted: candidate takeaways and the passages supporting them were surfaced across the full transcript set rather than assembled by hand. The participant-count check is what makes that defensible. Every candidate still had to clear the same three-way tagging against every participant before it could be elevated — so the tool could propose a pattern, but not confirm one.

Transcript conventions

All sessions were recorded and transcribed, then cleaned against a documented convention: filler words removed but never inside quotations; corrections notated with the original preserved; ambiguous inferences flagged rather than resolved silently; speech crossing task boundaries marked. Common speech-to-text errors were logged and corrected systematically — the transcription reliably mangled the domain vocabulary in the same ways, so the fixes could be applied consistently.

The convention was written first and applied with AI assistance — the filler and notation passes, and the recurring domain-vocabulary corrections. Having the rules fixed in advance is what let the work be delegated: the tool applied a documented convention rather than exercising judgment about what a participant meant.

Cleaned transcripts were treated as the source of truth and frozen once finalized.

A/B qualitative design

Two distinct participant groups per round, each experiencing one variant. This allowed direct comparison of comprehension, willingness, and mental model formation between framings — rather than only measuring change before and after within a single group.

What changed between rounds

Findings fed directly into the next round's stimulus, so each round tested a flow the previous round had shaped.

  • R1 → R2 CTA copy was revised and the enrollment prompt repositioned, so the offer read more clearly and arrived at a less disruptive point in the purchase. Supporting messaging was rewritten to communicate the cross-merchant benefit earlier.
  • R2 → R3 The two-step structure was made more visually distinct and terminology was standardized across the flow. Context was tightened rather than added to — enough to answer the questions participants were raising, without lengthening what they had to read at the decision point.

Outcome

Findings from this study directly informed:

  • The messaging hierarchy on the enrollment prompt screen
  • A decision to prioritize the second-purchase experience in value communication, rather than leading with security or speed claims at enrollment
  • Recommendations on where the enrollment prompt should sit in the journey — post-purchase rather than mid-checkout
  • Terminology standardization across the product, resolving inconsistent usage between related but distinct concepts
