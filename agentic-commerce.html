Agentic Commerce & Payment Authentication

February 2026

What are users' thoughts when it comes to AI-assisted purchases? A moderated qualitative study to understand users' needs, expectations, and concerns around AI-assisted commerce and payment authentication.

Role
Lead UX Researcher · Team of 3 researchers and a research manager
Methods
Moderated qualitative interviews · Counterbalanced scenario design · Cross-market synthesis
Scale
n = 24 · 6 per market · 90-minute remote sessions
Markets
US, Australia, Brazil, UK

Research objectives

Agentic commerce lets an AI agent search, decide, or buy on a user's behalf. To design an experience users would trust, we had to learn what they would delegate, what they would not, and what an agent would have to demonstrate before they would accept its help. The study was built around four questions:

  • How do consumers react to agentic commerce overall — the idea of delegating purchasing decisions to an AI agent?
  • How does passkey authentication fit into an agent experience, and what does it do for perceived security?
  • How do people respond to an agent purchasing across multiple merchants in one interaction?
  • How do they respond to pre-approving an agent to buy when conditions are met, without real-time input?

My role

  • Led full study design and moderation guide development — scenario selection, sequencing rationale, and counterbalancing logic
  • Moderated a share of the sessions — fieldwork was split across the team — and led synthesis
  • Worked with two research teammates on distributed section syntheses, each of us developing the themes, key takeaways, and recommendations for the sections we covered
  • Co-authored final report deck and presented to product and design stakeholders across two product teams (passkey and agentic)

How the study ran

All participants experienced enrollment first. The two subsequent scenarios — multi-merchant purchasing and conditional pre-approval — were counterbalanced across two groups of twelve. The two represent different degrees of delegation, and seeing the more autonomous one first could plausibly change how a user might read the verification steps in the other. Splitting the order meant that effect could be checked.

Participants were recruited for a baseline of digital and AI familiarity: shopping online at least monthly, holding a credit or debit card, using biometrics or a PIN to unlock their device, and some prior experience with AI chat tools.

What we found

The dominant orientation across all four markets was cautious interest. Participants could describe real appeal in agentic shopping, but also named the concerns and conditions under which they would not trust it. The tension was between convenience and control: an agent's promise is that it removes cognitive load; its risk is that it acts without the user's real-time judgment.

Participants generally sought control, transparency, collaboration, and creativity from the agent.

In practice, the line fell in a fairly consistent place. Participants were comfortable delegating narrow, low-stakes, well-specified purchases — replenishing a household staple, reordering a product they already knew. They were not comfortable delegating anything involving taste, judgment, or meaningful cost, where the "right" answer is subjective and an error is expensive to undo.

Passkey enrollment landed well, but user understanding could be improved. Participants responded positively and described the passkey as an extra layer of security. Three gaps surfaced consistently across sessions: what the passkey was actually doing, where the credential or biometric data lived, and who was providing the service. Where it did its clearest work was at the moment of authorization: participants expected the prompt, read its appearance as the system behaving the way a secure system should, and experienced the gesture as their own deliberate authorization rather than the agent acting alone. Its presence raised stated willingness to use agentic commerce.

Multi-merchant purchasing read as a genuine convenience gain. Participants immediately grasped that the agent could purchase from two merchants in one transaction, and expected the passkey authentication prompt when it came. The gap was the verification screen itself: it showed the total but not which item came from which merchant, so participants were approving a number they could not break down.

Pre-approval was the most appealing scenario, and the most fragile. Letting an agent buy once stated conditions are met addressed a burden participants recognized instantly. It was undercut by practical gaps: a window too short to feel like real delegation, no clarity on when funds would actually be committed, and no obvious way to manage a request already in flight.

Across the scenarios we tested, the through-line was the same: an agent earns delegation by being clear in what it is doing and what is left to the user. The moments that lost people were the ones where they could not see what they were agreeing to.

Outcome

The study informed the design decisions for agentic commerce with passkey authentication enrollment, multi-merchant purchasing, and conditional pre-approval through a set of specific recommendations. Both the agentic and passkey product teams took the findings forward, expanding on the designs and the direction for agentic commerce.

Conducted at a global payment network. Company and product names, participant details, and proprietary metrics have been omitted.
