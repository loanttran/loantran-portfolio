Case study 03

Agentic Commerce & Payment Security

What does someone need before they will let software spend their money without asking first? Twenty-four people across four countries, and an answer that was more consistent across markets — and more about control than security — than I expected going in.

Role
Lead Design Researcher · Team of 3 researchers and a research manager
Methods
Moderated qualitative interviews · Counterbalanced scenario design · Cross-market synthesis
Scale
n = 24 · 6 per market · 90-minute remote sessions · Ages 23–67
Markets
US, Australia, Brazil, UK · February 2026

The questions

  • How do consumers react to agentic commerce overall — the idea of delegating purchasing decisions to an AI agent?
  • How does payment passkey fit into an agent experience, and what does it do for perceived security?
  • How do people respond to an agent purchasing across multiple merchants in one interaction?
  • How do they respond to pre-approving an agent to buy when conditions are met, without real-time input?

My role

  • Led full study design and moderation guide development — scenario selection, sequencing rationale, and counterbalancing logic
  • Managed the counterbalanced protocol across the sample and analyzed observed order effects
  • Moderated sessions and led synthesis across all four countries
  • Collaborated with two research teammates on distributed session coding and cross-market theme development
  • Produced all themes, persona-level variation findings, and prioritized recommendations across four research areas
  • Authored the full report and presented to product, design, and leadership stakeholders

Design

Twenty-four participants across four markets, six per country, in 90-minute remote sessions. The protocol had to cover four distinct research areas in that time, across three scenarios, without the ordering of those scenarios contaminating the results.

All participants experienced enrollment first. The two subsequent scenarios — multi-merchant purchasing and conditional pre-approval — were counterbalanced across two groups of twelve.

Why counterbalance

The two scenarios represent different degrees of delegation. Experiencing the more autonomous model first plausibly changes how someone interprets the verification steps in the less autonomous one — making a fully sequential protocol quietly self-confirming.

Splitting the order lets that effect be detected rather than assumed absent. In a study this size, design discipline is what separates a finding from an anecdote.

Screening

Recruited for a baseline of digital and AI familiarity: shops online at least monthly, holds a credit or debit card, uses biometrics or a PIN to unlock their device, and has some prior experience with AI chat tools. The study was about what people who could plausibly use this actually think of it.

What I found

01

Cautious interest — and the tension underneath it

The dominant orientation in every market was cautious interest. Participants could articulate genuine appeal and, in the same breath, name the exact conditions under which they would not trust it. This was not ambivalence; it was a coherent position.

The core tension is between convenience and control. The promise of an agent is that it removes cognitive load. The risk is that it acts without the user's real-time judgment. Those are the same property, described from two directions — which is why the appeal and the anxiety scale together rather than trading off.

02

Eight themes, and two pairs that are usually collapsed

Cross-session analysis produced eight themes: security and data privacy, privacy, control and autonomy, confidence, convenience, risk, autonomy, and accuracy.

Two distinctions in that list did real work. Security and privacy are not the same concern — security was about breaches and who holds the data; privacy was about visibility, what is seen by agent platforms, merchants, and advertisers. And risk is not the same as security — participants assessed the risk of the agent simply being wrong (wrong item, wrong size, wrong timing) as entirely separate from the risk of fraud.

Collapsing either pair would have produced a tidier theme list and a worse set of recommendations, because the mitigations are different in each case.

03

The comfort zone is narrow, and the boundary is subjectivity

Participants were comfortable delegating low-value or recurring purchases — replenishing household staples, reordering a known product, anything where the parameters are narrow and easy to specify.

They were uncomfortable with high-value purchases and with anything involving personal style or taste: clothing, gifts, decisions where the "right" answer is subjective.

The dividing line is not price. It is whether a correct answer exists independently of the buyer's judgment. An agent can be trusted to execute a decision; it is not trusted to make one.

04

Three preconditions, stated almost identically across four markets

Asked what they would need before genuinely using agentic commerce:

  • Collaborative interaction. The agent asks, confirms, and checks in rather than acting unilaterally. One framing recurred across markets: working with me, not for me.
  • Security and data privacy. Explicit explanation of what data is used, where it is stored, and who can access it.
  • Control over the experience. The ability to set limits, cancel, review, and modify — an active relationship rather than a passive one.

The cross-market consistency here was the surprise. I expected more variation between markets with different e-commerce maturity and different relationships to financial institutions. The preconditions were close to identical.

05

Biometric authentication reads as authorship, not as security overhead

This was the most useful finding for design. Participants rated the passkey enrollment experience highly on convenience and security, with trustworthiness trailing slightly behind — and the qualitative data explained the gap.

At the moment of verification, most participants already expected a biometric prompt, and its appearance confirmed the expectation rather than interrupting them. It did not read as the system doubting them. It read as the moment their intention became real — a concrete gesture that made the purchase theirs rather than the agent's.

That is a different function from security. In a context where the central anxiety is loss of control, an authentication step is not friction to be minimized — it is the mechanism by which a user re-enters a process they have delegated. Removing it would make the experience faster and less trusted.

06

Enthusiasm rested on a shallow model of the mechanism

Participants responded positively to passkey enrollment — but the enthusiasm was built on a vague understanding. The spontaneous mental model was "an extra layer of security," used confidently while masking real uncertainty about what was actually happening.

Three knowledge gaps appeared consistently across sessions and markets:

  • What is it doing? Some thought it replaced their card number, some thought it was a second password, some thought it was bound to their phone. It resolved to something vague but reassuring.
  • Where is my data stored? Whether credentials live on the device, at the bank, or elsewhere was genuinely unknown to most. They cared, and could not find out from the flow.
  • Who is providing this? The identity of the service provider was not clear from the enrollment experience, and participants noticed — being asked to trust something whose provenance is unclear produces a specific kind of unease.

Positive sentiment resting on an inaccurate model is fragile. It holds until the first moment something unexpected happens, and then there is nothing underneath it.

The through-line. Across all three scenarios, the presence of a strong authentication gesture increased stated willingness to use agentic commerce. Its role in this context is not really transactional security — it is confidence infrastructure.

Which suggests a general principle for delegated systems: the design problem is not minimizing the number of times a user has to intervene. It is making sure the interventions that remain feel like authorship rather than interruption.

Recommendations

Positioning:

  • Frame the experience as familiar and efficient rather than novel and futuristic — people need a conceptual bridge from behavior they already trust
  • Provide clear product detail and variety within the agent experience, supporting confidence that the agent has understood intent accurately
  • Use collaborative language throughout — confirm, review, approve — rather than language implying autonomous action

Enrollment:

  • Add a plainly written "how it works" section naming the mechanism
  • State explicitly who provides the service, and where data is and is not stored

Delegation and control:

  • Show enough detail at the point of approval that users can decompose what they are approving, rather than confirming a summary they cannot verify
  • Provide a visible management path for anything the agent has been pre-authorized to do — participants split between managing it in-chat and via a dashboard, but both preferences point to the same underlying need for accessible control
  • Let users opt in or out of a notification when the agent completes a delegated action, closing the loop on "set and forget"

Conducted at a global payment network. Internal product names, proprietary business data, brand strategy findings, unreleased product specifics, and team member names have been omitted. Figures describe study design only; findings are described qualitatively.
