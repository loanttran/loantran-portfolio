Case study 03

Agentic Commerce & Payment Authentication

What are users' thoughts when it comes to AI-assisted purchases? A moderated qualitative study to understand users' needs, expectations, and concerns around AI-assisted commerce and payment authentication.

Role
Lead UX Researcher · Team of 3 researchers and a research manager
Methods
Moderated qualitative interviews · Counterbalanced scenario design · Cross-market synthesis
Scale
n = 24 · 6 per market · 90-minute remote sessions · Ages 23–67
Markets
US, Australia, Brazil, UK · February 2026

The questions

  • How do consumers react to agentic commerce overall — the idea of delegating purchasing decisions to an AI agent?
  • How does payment passkey fit into an agent experience, and what does it do for perceived security?
  • How do people respond to an agent purchasing across multiple merchants in one interaction?
  • How do they respond to pre-approving an agent to buy when conditions are met, without real-time input?

My role

  • Led full study design and moderation guide development — scenario selection, sequencing rationale, and counterbalancing logic
  • Managed the counterbalanced protocol across the sample and analyzed observed order effects
  • Moderated a share of the sessions — fieldwork was split across the team — and led synthesis across all four markets
  • Worked with two research teammates on distributed session coding and cross-market theme development; the eight themes were developed together
  • Produced the persona-level variation findings and prioritized recommendations across four research areas
  • Authored the full report and presented to product, design, and leadership stakeholders

Design

Twenty-four participants across four markets, six per country, in 90-minute remote sessions. The protocol had to cover four distinct research areas in that time, across three scenarios, without the ordering of those scenarios contaminating the results.

All participants experienced enrollment first. The two subsequent scenarios — multi-merchant purchasing and conditional pre-approval — were counterbalanced across two groups of twelve.

Why counterbalance

The two scenarios represent different degrees of delegation. Experiencing the more autonomous model first plausibly changes how someone interprets the verification steps in the less autonomous one — making a fully sequential protocol quietly self-confirming.

Splitting the order lets that effect be detected rather than assumed absent. In a study this size, design discipline is what separates a finding from an anecdote.

Screening

Recruited for a baseline of digital and AI familiarity: shops online at least monthly, holds a credit or debit card, uses biometrics or a PIN to unlock their device, and has some prior experience with AI chat tools. The study was about what people who could plausibly use this actually think of it.

Recommendations

Positioning:

  • Frame the experience as familiar and efficient rather than novel and futuristic — people need a conceptual bridge from behavior they already trust
  • Provide clear product detail and variety within the agent experience, supporting confidence that the agent has understood intent accurately
  • Use collaborative language throughout — confirm, review, approve — rather than language implying autonomous action

Enrollment:

  • Add a plainly written "how it works" section naming the mechanism
  • State explicitly who provides the service, and where data is and is not stored

Delegation and control:

  • Show enough detail at the point of approval that users can decompose what they are approving, rather than confirming a summary they cannot verify
  • Provide a visible management path for anything the agent has been pre-authorized to do — participants split between managing it in-chat and via a dashboard, but both preferences point to the same underlying need for accessible control
  • Let users opt in or out of a notification when the agent completes a delegated action, closing the loop on "set and forget"

Outcome

No build followed directly. The study was aimed at direction rather than execution, and that is where it landed — it changed the design approach to two moments in particular: enrollment, and the point at which a user hands a decision over to the agent. Both were reframed in light of the research before design work continued.

At the strategic level, it shaped how the organization framed agentic commerce as a design problem — which questions were treated as the ones worth answering first, and what would have to be true before the experience was worth building at all.

It also outlived its brief. The report circulated past the stakeholders it was presented to and became the internal reference on the subject, picked up by teams that had not commissioned it.
