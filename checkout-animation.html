Security Animation at Checkout Survey

April 2025

An A/B/C between-subjects test of two security animations against no animation at all, fielded simultaneously in two markets. The goal was to understand whether the animations increased users' trust in the checkout experience and their likelihood to complete a purchase.

Role
Lead UX Researcher
Methods
Quantitative survey · A/B/C between-subjects · Significance testing
Scale
n = 1,800 · 900 per market · 300 per condition per market · 8-minute instrument
Markets
US and India

Research objectives

The moment a payment is processing is dead time the user has to sit through, and the proposal was to fill it with an animation that signals the transaction is secure. That raised a question of whether a security cue at checkout could actually change how the experience feels, and if so, whether it should carry the network's own branded symbol or a generic padlock any shopper already knows how to read. The study was built around four questions:

  • Do checkout security animations improve perceived security, trustworthiness, and purchase likelihood versus no animation?
  • Which animation performs better — and does the answer differ by market?
  • Why do consumers choose one over the other?
  • Does prior familiarity with the underlying payment concept affect preference?

My role

  • Designed the A/B/C between-subjects instrument
  • Coordinated with brand, design, and product teams on stimulus development
  • Built the survey in aytm and tested it for usability and technical performance in conjunction with survey platform vendor
  • Managed dual-market fielding with consistent quality controls across both panels
  • Shared the statistical significance testing across conditions with the team
  • Co-authored the comparative report with research manager and presented findings to the design and product team

Design

Three checkout flows compared in a between-subjects design. Each participant saw and rated one randomly assigned flow, then viewed all three side by side to state an overall preference — so the study captured both an uncontaminated first impression and a considered comparison. The two markets were chosen for contrast rather than convenience: most US respondents were unfamiliar with tokenization as a payments concept, while most respondents in India already recognized it, which made any dependence on prior knowledge measurable.

  • A Branded token animation A branded payment-security icon appears during payment processing, representing the network's tokenization technology.
  • B Padlock animation A padlock closes during payment processing — a universal, symbol-based security indicator with no brand attached.
  • C No animation Standard checkout with no animated security indicator. The control.

Measures

  • Perceived security of the checkout experience
  • Trustworthiness of the checkout
  • Likelihood of future purchase
  • Animation appeal (animation conditions only)
  • Open-ended reasoning for the preference stated

What we found

In the US, both animations beat the control, and the lift held across all three measures.

The padlock won on universality.

The padlock led the branded token on every US measure, and the open-ends explained why: participants read it instantly and without instruction — "encrypted," "safe to enter my card," "the site is protected." The branded token drew comments closer to logo recognition than to a security claim — consistent with how few US respondents knew the concept going in.

India, with its higher familiarity baseline, flattened the result: no concept separated significantly from any other on any measure.

Outcome

The interest in a security animation was justified. Both concepts outperformed no animation on security, trust, and repurchase intent in the US — meaningfully, not just directionally.

What the survey could not answer is whether the attitudinal data would hold. Participants rated a simulated checkout with nothing at stake; the measures were self-reported, and the preference exercise asked them to compare three flows they would never see side by side in a real purchase scenario. Findings were handed off as the starting hypotheses for research with a live merchant to test the padlock animation against behavior, not stated intent. The lift should show up in completion and abandonment on real transactions, or it remains a survey artifact.

Conducted at a global payment network. Company and product names, participant details, and proprietary metrics have been omitted.
