Case study 04

Checkout Security Animation A/B

Does a security animation at checkout actually make people feel safer — and if so, does it matter which one? Fielded simultaneously in two markets chosen because they disagreed about the answer before the study started.

Role
Lead Design Researcher
Methods
Quantitative survey · A/B/C between-subjects · Significance testing
Scale
n = 1,800 · 900 per market · 300 per condition per market · 8-minute instrument
Markets
US and India · April 2025

The questions

  • Do checkout security animations improve perceived security, trustworthiness, and purchase likelihood versus no animation?
  • Which animation performs better — and does the answer differ by market?
  • Why do consumers choose one over the other?
  • Does prior familiarity with the underlying payment concept affect preference?

Design

Three checkout flows compared in a between-subjects design. Each participant saw and rated one randomly assigned flow, then viewed all three side by side to state an overall preference — so the study captured both an uncontaminated first impression and a considered comparison.

  • A Branded token animation A branded payment-security icon appears during payment processing, representing the network's tokenization technology.
  • B Padlock animation A padlock closes during payment processing — a universal, symbol-based security indicator with no brand attached.
  • C No animation Standard checkout with no animated security indicator. The control.

The dual-market decision

The two markets were selected deliberately, not for convenience. The US and India have close to opposite baselines on familiarity with tokenization as a payments concept — the large majority of US respondents were unfamiliar with it, while the majority of India respondents already recognized it.

That contrast turns a confound into an instrument. If the branded animation depends on prior knowledge to work, running it in only one market would hide that; running it in both makes the dependency measurable.

Measures

  • Perceived security of the checkout experience
  • Trustworthiness of the checkout
  • Likelihood of future purchase
  • Animation appeal (animation conditions only)
  • Open-ended reasoning for the preference stated

What I found

01

Animation beats no animation — consistently, in both markets

A large majority in both the US and India preferred some animation over none, at strikingly similar rates across two very different markets. In the US, both animation conditions significantly outperformed the control on perceived security, trustworthiness, and likelihood of future purchase.

This is the finding that justified the investment. It is also the cleanest: a stable, cross-market result on the question that actually determines whether to build the thing at all.

02

The lift comes from animation presence, not from which animation

In the US, the difference between the two animation conditions was not statistically significant on the rated measures. Both beat the control; neither reliably beat the other on how the checkout felt.

Reporting a null result clearly is worth as much here as reporting a positive one. It tells the team the decision between the two animations should be made on other grounds — because this measure won't settle it.

03

In direct comparison, the padlock won the US clearly

When participants saw all three side by side, the padlock was the single most preferred option in the US by a significant margin — ahead of the branded token, and ahead of "either animation."

The open ends explain it. Padlock choosers overwhelmingly cited security and safety, with simplicity and symbol familiarity behind it. The padlock's advantage is that "lock means secure" requires no knowledge of payment infrastructure whatsoever. Token choosers also led with security, but were distinctly more likely to cite the animation being informative or descriptive — a benefit that only exists if you already know what it is describing.

04

India showed no single winner

In India, the padlock held the highest raw numbers across measures, but the differences between the three conditions were not statistically significant. Indian respondents were broadly positive about all three checkout experiences, and the most-selected preference option was "either animation" rather than a specific one.

A market where the choice doesn't matter is strategically useful information: it means flexibility, not failure.

05

Familiarity narrowed the gap but did not close it

The obvious explanation for the padlock's US win is that people don't know what a payment token is. So I checked it against the subgroup that did.

Among token-familiar US respondents, the padlock still won — the gap narrowed, but it did not close. That matters, because it means the padlock's advantage is not purely a knowledge deficit that education will erase. It is grounded in mental model. At the same time, familiarity does correlate with token preference, which points to a genuine educational trajectory: as awareness grows through product exposure, the branded animation's competitive position should strengthen.

Interest in learning more about the underlying product was dramatically higher in India than in the US — which captures the entire familiarity difference between the two markets in a single measure.

Outcome

  • Animation investment justified. Both conditions outperformed no animation on security, trust, and repurchase intent in the US — meaningfully, not just directionally.
  • Padlock recommended for US launch. Its universal symbolism resonated regardless of prior knowledge, and it held its advantage even among the token-aware.
  • India is flexible. High familiarity and no significant winner opened the door to a market-differentiated strategy rather than a global mandate.
  • The branded token has an educational path. Its weakness in the US is an awareness story with a plausible trajectory, not a design failure.

My role

  • Designed the A/B/C between-subjects instrument
  • Coordinated with brand, design, and product teams on stimulus development — specifically the three video conditions tested
  • Managed dual-market fielding with consistent quality controls across both panels
  • Ran statistical significance testing on all measures between conditions
  • Authored the comparative report and presented findings to the cross-functional team

Conducted at a global payment network. Internal product names and proprietary business data have been omitted. Figures describe study design only; findings are described qualitatively.
