Case study 01

Payment Verification Flow — Usability Testing

Three rounds of unmoderated testing on a biometric checkout enrollment experience. The most consequential finding was structural, and slightly inconvenient: the moment you have to convince someone is the one moment you cannot show them why.

Role
Lead UX Researcher
Methods
Unmoderated usability testing · A/B qual · Transcript analysis · Participant-count verification
Scale
3 rounds · ~10 participants per round · UK and US · Remote
Output
Round reports with six verified takeaways each

The study

The product team was testing a passkey-based payment verification flow — a checkout experience where a cardholder enrolls a biometric to replace one-time passcodes on future purchases. Two framings of the same flow were tested side by side: one using standard enrollment language, one reframed around the passkey concept itself.

Three questions were asked of every variant, every round:

  • Why are participants likely or unlikely to set up verification?
  • Did the authentication experience meet their expectations?
  • Where did they hesitate, and where did they have questions or concerns?

The two-purchase structure

Each participant completed two tasks: buy a toaster as a first-time user, which triggers the enrollment prompt — then buy a bicycle at a different merchant, which tests whether the enrolled passkey works seamlessly and whether they notice.

The second purchase was the point. Watching the passkey work automatically at a different merchant was the moment most participants understood what they had signed up for. Designing that moment into the protocol is what made the study's central finding visible at all.

My role

  • Designed the three-round study, including the A/B qualitative structure and the two-purchase task sequence that made the central finding observable at all
  • Developed the participant-count verification method and applied it to every candidate takeaway across all three rounds
  • Wrote the transcript cleaning convention, including the systematic corrections for speech-to-text errors that recurred in the domain vocabulary
  • Wrote the discussion guide and set up the unmoderated test across UK and US participants
  • Authored each round’s report — six verified takeaways per round — and carried findings into the next round’s stimulus
  • Presented recommendations that informed enrollment prompt placement, messaging hierarchy, and terminology standardization across the product

Method

Participant-count verification

Every candidate takeaway was checked against the full participant set before it could be elevated to a key finding. Each was tagged three ways:

  • Supports — participants with clear, explicit evidence
  • Partially supports — participants with indirect or weaker evidence
  • Does not support — participants who contradicted it

This exists to prevent impressionistic synthesis. A finding that sounds compelling but rests on one or two vivid participants does not get presented as a pattern — and the vivid sessions are exactly the ones that stay with you.

Transcript conventions

All sessions were recorded and transcribed, then cleaned against a documented convention: filler words removed but never inside quotations; corrections notated with the original preserved; ambiguous inferences flagged rather than resolved silently; speech crossing task boundaries marked. Common speech-to-text errors were logged and corrected systematically — the transcription reliably mangled the domain vocabulary in the same ways, so the fixes could be applied consistently.

Cleaned transcripts were treated as the source of truth and frozen once finalized.

A/B qualitative design

Two distinct participant groups per round, each experiencing one variant. This allowed direct comparison of comprehension, willingness, and mental model formation between framings — rather than only measuring change before and after within a single group.

What I found

01

Willingness was predicted by repeat-use intent, not by the flow

Whether someone was willing to set up verification had almost nothing to do with which variant they saw. The deciding factor was whether they expected to return to the merchant. Participants framed it explicitly as an investment — if they would never use the site again, there was no reason to leave biometric details behind. Consistent across both groups and all three rounds.

02

Mid-checkout placement amplified existing hesitancy

For participants already inclined to skip, interrupting the purchase made it worse — they described being focused on completing the transaction and not wanting to stop. Participants who were already willing moved through setup without friction. The placement didn't create hesitancy; it amplified it in people who were on the fence. That distinction matters for what you do about it.

03

Most participants did not register two distinct steps

Many completed the one-time passcode and the passkey setup as a single undifferentiated event. Asked afterward whether they had noticed two separate verification steps, several were surprised — some hadn't registered the second one at all. Among those who did notice, comprehension of the difference varied considerably.

04

Some assumed verification was retailer-specific

Participants with prior experience of a card issuer's trusted-merchant feature initially assumed this setup was store-specific rather than card-level and cross-merchant. Both self-corrected — one by rereading the screen carefully, the other when the second purchase demonstrated continuity across retailers. The correction happened, but it took effort that clearer network framing would have made unnecessary on first exposure.

05

Mental models varied, but outcome predictions were more reliable

Asked what they had signed up for, participants ranged from precise to essentially empty. But asked what to expect next time, noticeably more of them answered correctly than could explain the mechanism. For several, the practical outcome was intuitive even where the underlying concept was not — which suggests that comprehension of the mechanism may be the wrong thing to optimize for.

06

The second purchase delivered clarity the enrollment screen could not

The most consistent finding across all three rounds. Participants who had been confused, hesitant, or indifferent during enrollment reported that the second purchase made everything make sense. One participant who rated their likelihood of setting it up near the bottom of the scale reversed entirely to enthusiasm after experiencing the streamlined second checkout. Another, who had fundamentally misunderstood what she had signed up for, said she was confused initially but understood after doing it a second time.

Why this one mattered most. The implication is structural, not cosmetic. The enrollment moment is the wrong place to convince anyone — the proof of value comes from experiencing the benefit, and that experience can only happen after they have already enrolled. This creates a persuasion gap that copy and UI cannot fully close, no matter how well written.

Which reframes the design problem. If enrollment can't carry the argument, then the job of the enrollment screen is to lower the cost of saying yes — not to justify it.

What changed between rounds

Findings fed directly into the next round's stimulus, so each round tested a flow the previous round had shaped.

  • R1 → R2 The enrollment prompt was repositioned in response to the finding that mid-checkout placement compounded hesitancy, and messaging was updated to communicate the cross-merchant benefit earlier — addressing the retailer-specificity confusion at its source.
  • R2 → R3 The two-step structure was made more visually distinct, addressing the finding that participants were not registering two separate events. Terminology was standardized to reduce the label confusion that had emerged in the previous round.

Outcome

Findings from this study directly informed:

  • The messaging hierarchy on the enrollment prompt screen
  • A decision to prioritize the second-purchase experience in value communication, rather than leading with security or speed claims at enrollment
  • Recommendations on where the enrollment prompt should sit in the journey — post-purchase rather than mid-checkout
  • Terminology standardization across the product, resolving inconsistent usage between related but distinct concepts

Conducted at a global payment network. Company names, internal product names, participant names, and proprietary metrics have been omitted. Figures describe study design only; findings are described qualitatively.
