01

Introduction

I had never considered that search engines were not serving users as well as they could, but in this class, I realized how strong of a mental model the Google search engine had become and how most of us have accepted the simple relation of inputting a query and receiving a page of text-based results. I began to consider how we can design our search systems to do more and employ features that encouraged exploratory search and browsing to enhance learning and discovery. I was also interested in our class discussion on visualization and how that could be integrated into the search process to enhance user experience. As I wondered about the nature of search systems, I began thinking about Pinterest and its visual search results. Through the lens of exploratory search, I wanted to know what makes Pinterest appealing to users, what it has to offer as a search engine, and the features it provided. I was interested in a search engine that utilizes images and was a platform for everyday information seeking behavior, rather than the academic or work-based searches that tend to be more defined at the start of the information-seeking process.

02

System

Pinterest is a social media platform based on collecting and sharing images. Its emphasis on visual discovery and the creation of “boards” (simulating bulletin boards) allows users to search, select, classify, organize, and curate information over time.

On Pinterest, users input search queries and browse results like a normal search engine, but these results can then be saved onto a board created by the user and shared. The main surrogate of Pinterest search results are images, making the SERP visually appealing. Image search results invites a subjective factor of interpretation and the user’s own personal values, beliefs, and aesthetics when determining the relevance of the image to the search query. Clicking on an image takes the user to a new page and presents a URL to where the image is located (i.e. an external website), the image title, description, social interactions, and the last user who posted the image to a board. Beneath this information are more image results related to the selected image and defined as, “More like this,” creating a network of shared images across a number of users interested in the same or similar topics. This network of related images encourages continuous browsing and cataloging, and places users in an environment where discovery is more fulfilling than single answers.

03

Methods

Subjects

The population this pilot study focused on is regular Pinterest users, both novice and experienced. Originally, I planned to recruit six participants, three which were experienced and three which were novice users of Pinterest. However in my actual recruitment, I was only able to recruit four experienced users and two novice users. These participants were people that I knew and who had some degree of familiarity with Pinterest. They were identified as experienced or novice users in the pre-session questionnaire. Each study session was an hour long and participants were recruited through a general social media post, as well as through email.

Research Design

The criteria for this study were user interest, user engagement, and usefulness. Measures that were considered included browsing behavior, the visual aesthetic of the Pinterest SERP, and interaction with features (pinning to a board, following a URL to an external page, adding comments, following other boards/users, sharing results, etc.).

To collect data for this study, I used two questionnaires: one before the study session and one after. For the study session itself, I used three search tasks to explore user engagement in terms of exploratory search and browsing, as well as use of Pinterest features. Each task was followed by a set of post-tasks questions and after all three tasks were completed, participants were asked a set of exit interview questions.

Pre-Session Questionnaire

This initial questionnaire was used to gather demographic information and gauge the experience of the participant in regards to using Pinterest. Additional information that was gathered was: what do participants use Pinterest for, which features they used most, and how satisfied are they with their current experience.

  • Full Name [open-ended]
  • What is your age (18-24 years, 25-34 years, 35-44 years, 45-55 years, 55 years or older)?
  • What gender do you identify with? Write N/A if you prefer not to say. [open-ended]
  • What is your occupation? [open-ended]
  • How long have you been using Pinterest (0-1 month, 2-6 months, 7-12 months, more than a year, other)?
  • How often do you use Pinterest (everyday, a few times a week, about once a week, a few times a month, once a month, less than once a month)?
  • How much time do you spend on Pinterest per session (less than 10 minutes, about 30 minutes, about an hour, more than an hour)?
  • What do you use Pinterest for? [open-ended]
  • Which features do you use the most? [open-ended]
  • How satisfied are you with your experience on Pinterest (not at all satisfied, slightly satisfied, moderately satisfied, very satisfied, extremely satisfied)?

Study: Tasks, Post-Task Questions, Exit Questions

Task [1]: Once it’s safe to travel again, you and your friend would like to take a trip to Denmark. Create a board and add three day-trip ideas to show your friend later.

Task [2]: Your friend is putting you in charge of decorating the tables for her wedding. You know that she wants the color scheme to be green, rose, and white. Create a board and add three decoration ideas to show your friend.

Task [3]: Mother’s Day is coming up and you want to purchase a card for your mom. You think Pinterest will have some small businesses or individual creators who make unique, handmade cards, so you want to find them and follow their account.

Post-Task Questions:

  • Have you searched something like this in the past?
  • How was it getting started (easy, hard, somewhere in-between)?
  • How was it browsing? What was your thought process?
  • How did you make your selection(s)?
  • With “1” being “Not at all satisfied” and “5” being “Extremely satisfied,” how satisfied are you with the search results you received?
  • If this were a real situation, would you have…
    • [Task 1 and 2] Created a board and showed the board to your friend?
    • [Task 3] Followed the account?
  • [Task 3 only] How often do you follow another Pinterest user’s account (always, often, occasionally, rarely, never)?

Exit Questions:

  • Generally, what first catches your attention on the search results page? Were you drawn to the text, image, or other component?
  • How satisfied are you with your experience on Pinterest (not at all satisfied, slightly satisfied, moderately satisfied, very satisfied, extremely satisfied)? Could you tell me more about why you feel that way?
  • What is the best feature/quality/thing about Pinterest?
  • What is the worst feature/quality/thing about Pinterest?
  • Would you evaluate the materials/search results you found on Pinterest differently than you would on a search engine like Google?
  • Will you continue to use Pinterest in the future?

Post-Session Questionnaire

This post-session questionnaire allowed participants to evaluate their Pinterest search experience during the session. The participants were asked to rate their experience on a five-point scale. Questions included:

  • On a scale of 1-5, overall, how difficult was it to start your search tasks? (1= not difficult, 5= very difficult).
  • On a scale of 1-5, overall, how difficult was it to understand the search results? (1= not difficult, 5= very difficult).
  • On a scale of 1-5, overall, how much did this search make you think? (1= no thinking, 5= a great deal of thinking).
  • On a scale of 1-5, overall, how difficult was it to make decisions on which search results were useful? (1= not difficult, 5= very difficult).
  • How would you describe your browsing behavior? [open-ended]
  • On a scale of 1-5, overall, how often do you know what you’re looking for before you browse? (1= never, 5 = always).
  • On a scale of 1-5, overall, do you use all the features on Pinterest? (1= I use none of the features, 5 = I use all of the features).
  • What features do you use most often? [open-ended]
  • What features do you use least often? [open-ended]
  • On a scale of 1-5, overall, how likely would it be for you to recommend Pinterest to a friend? (1= very unlikely, 5 = very likely).

Data Collection Procedures

The pre-session and post-session questionnaires were created using Google Form and responses were collected on two separate Google spreadsheets. All study data, quantitative and qualitative, was collected in a Google spreadsheet. This template is replicated in the “Appendices” at the end of this paper.

From the following pre-session questionnaire, I was able to identify that four participants (1-4) were experienced users of Pinterest based on the fact that they had more than one year of experience and used the site a few times a week to once a month, for less than 10 minutes to 30 minutes per session. Despite the small sample size, there were some general differences observed between experienced and novice users of Pinterest. Their experience with the platform features and search engine came to affect the number of search queries used, engagement in exploratory search, time spent browsing, and particularly, attention to creating and naming boards.

Pre-Session Questionnaire

From the questionnaire, five out of the six participants were moderately satisfied to very satisfied with their experience on Pinterest. One participant, a novice user, stated they were not satisfied at all. This was a good point of exploration when the study was conducted and the participant was able to show their use of Pinterest and talk more about their expectations.

In general, participants used Pinterest for topic-related searches: recipes, crafts, fashion, trip-planning, home decor, mood boards, quotes, affirmations, jokes, hair and beauty, phone backgrounds, gift ideas, and limited-edition items.

Aside from searching within those range of topics, participants also used Pinterest to save images from other sites using the Google Chrome extension and one participant used Pinterest to promote their Instagram reels. Primarily, the participants mentioned the ability to organize their pins/interest as being the main feature they use.

The pre-session questionnaire gave me an opportunity to quickly evaluate the tasks I had prepared for the study. The three task topics are related to trip-planning, wedding decorations, and a gift idea. These seemed to align with some of the identified topics of interest.

For the first task, the search queries usually started with variations of “Denmark travel” and “Denmark trip.” Three of the four experienced users looked at external links as they were searching, taking time to read the blogs associated to check for credibility. They were interested in blogs written by real people who had traveled to Denmark and wanted to find out more information on the places being recommended. All participants expressed that they didn’t know a lot about Denmark. In general, the participants were interest in popular places to see in Denmark, the “10 best things to do,” and food. Some tried to actually plan an itinerary for each day, but most collected three pins and saved them to a board. Participants also saved pins from boards already created for by other users. A few of the experienced users considered pins that were related to transit systems and currency. Overall, the participants were satisfied with their search results, but did not feel like they would want to plan a trip using Pinterest. Although there was attention to the visual image of the pin, the participants were more interested in text-based information for this task. Both experienced and novice users talked about using Google along with their trip-planning on Pinterest.

The second task seemed to embody the nature of what Pinterest can offer as a social media platform and search engine. Participants were looking at table decoration ideas for a wedding and it brought on comments about looking at images over searching for textual information (which many wanted to do for the first task). Because wedding ideas are widely explored and there’s a lot of content on it, the participants were able to use suggested search queries and the four experienced participants mentioned that they liked looking at color schemes.

This was the most ideal task for participants. It didn’t require the participants to leave the Pinterest site and was the task that generated the most search queries from each. Participants were interested in the color scheme presented by the scenario and used query suggestions like “centerpiece” and “tablescape” that they wouldn’t have thought of on their own. The name of the board mattered most in this task because it belonged to their friend and they wanted to clearly define the theme of the wedding. The difference between experienced and novice users is less obvious here and each of the participants mentioned that “weddings” was a well-searched and well-populated topic on Pinterest. The only male participant cited that he used Pinterest to get ideas for his brother’s groom gift.

For this task, participants also needed to use more search queries because the main results that came up in association to defining the wedding colors (a variation of “green,” “rose,” and/or “white” with “wedding”) usually brought up flowers. From there, each of the participants had to further define their search to find table decorations that they felt their friend would like and that were unique and interesting. This task was a success in figuring out where Pinterest excels as a search engine within a topic that a lot of users come to Pinterest for. During this task, there was mention of how satisfying it was to look at color schemes, and because table decorations can be related to home décor, many of the participants brought up using Pinterest to inspire furniture and home renovations.

The second task was also where the participants were the most decisive in their selection criteria. Focusing primarily on the pins, they were quickly able to determine what they liked and didn’t like without having to move to external links. They were most engaged and pinned more than what asked so that the friend would have multiple options to look at. It captured the process of using search queries to discover ideas and save search results/pins to a board. One experienced user even added notes to their board and actually create a checklist for other table decorations that could be included. Another experienced participant shared that she had been invited to her friend’s Pinterest board that was focused on wedding planning, introducing a collaborative feature in which various Pinterest users can add pins to a shared board. Only one participant was not satisfied with the search results they received, and they were among the participants that spent less than three minutes on the task. They were also the only participant who said they would not create a board on Pinterest to gather wedding decoration ideas.

The third task was the least successful in terms of user interest, user engagement, and usefulness. Even participants that spent the highest amount of time on the task were not necessarily satisfied with their search results and only three of the five participants actually chose to follow another user’s account.

This task revealed that rather than shopping on Pinterest, users would rather shop on Etsy, which has affiliated links and advertisements on Pinterest. In general, the participants reported that they would rather use Pinterest to get ideas and inspiration for homemade cards than look for cards to purchase. Even more unlikely was their willingness to follow another user’s account. The participants mostly use Pinterest for their personal creation of boards and collection of pins, and are rarely involved in community aspects such as the social features offered by Pinterest: following accounts and leaving comments or reactions on other pins. The participants also made it clear that they are unlikely to follow strangers. They tended to only follow users who they knew offline or through other social media platforms, so even though many people use Pinterest, and they constantly re-pin images from other users, they are often not connected to these other users.

Exit Questions

After the participants completed the three tasks, they were asked a set of exit interview questions. These questions were meant to allow the participant to evaluate Pinterest as a whole, especially in understanding the pin image as a surrogate. In this case, although the image serves as the surrogate for an external link, it is actually the image that matters most to participants—not the external link where it came from. When using Pinterest, participants have reported that they tend to stay on the site and browse through their homepage/feed rather than read or follow external links. Each commented on the personalization the feed generates based on their previous searches—to the point that if the participant went on Pinterest to browse, they wouldn’t even need to put in a query unless they were looking for something specific. It was rare for participants to use Pinterest when they know what they’re looking for. Usually, they used Pinterest to explore and discovery pins on their homepage and pin what they find aesthetically pleasing.

Among the best features, the participants spoke a lot about the ability to organize their pins in boards that they created and named. Many mentioned the visual aspect of Pinterest being an appealing factor in why they use Pinterest, and that the system is able to generate recommendations that meets what the user is interested in. Surprisingly, two of the participants talked about the “algorithm” and how good it was. The worst feature identified by the participants is advertisements that are now populating the Pinterest search results. Participants commented on how, sometimes, the ads were not even relevant to the search query and that it was obviously a paid ad.

In relation to Google, all participants said that they evaluated the search results found on Pinterest differently than search results on Google. They reported that users go to Pinterest for its visual quality, to discovery appealing images, and to organize images that they liked based on their interests, preferences, and aesthetics. Most participants were satisfied to very satisfied with their experience on Pinterest. Only one novice user was slightly satisfied, but said that she would not use Pinterest in the future. Five out of the six participants would use Pinterest again.

Post-Session Questionnaire

The post-session questionnaire gave participants the opportunity to rate the ease or difficulty of their search experience during the session. It also asked them some open-ended questions about how they would describe their browsing behavior, the features that they use, and whether they would recommend Pinterest to a friend. In general, participants did not find the search tasks to be difficult and they were able to understand the search results well. Most participants (four out of the six) reported that it was easy for them to make selections for each of the tasks and they did not have to think very hard. Overall, in describing their browsing behavior on Pinterest, participants said their behavior was “casual” and “recreational.” Primarily, Pinterest was used to get ideas and inspire discovery and creativity, while at other times, browsing could be “mindless.” Four of the six participants reported that the feature they used the most was creating a board, while the features used least often were much more varied: participants wrote that they didn’t often make purchases on Pinterest, follow other users, use the notes feature, look at the “Today” button, or use the Pinterest web browser. Overall, four of the six participants would “likely” to “very likely” recommend Pinterest to a friend.

05

Discussion

Interested in the visual component of Pinterest and how that can deepen exploratory search, I drew from Marchionini’s definition that, “exploratory search can be used to describe an information-seeking problem context that is open-ended, persistent, and multi-faceted; and to describe information-seeking processes that are opportunistic, iterative, and multi-tactical” (2006). Marchionini further explained that, “In the second sense [of the definition]… exploratory tactics are used in all manner of information-seeking and reflect seeker preferences and experience as much as the goal” (2006). Although this pilot study is too small to explore the many facets of exploratory search, I was interested in exploratory search as a process, and specifically Pinterest as a setting for exploratory search because of its search engine and visual search results. White and Roth state in “Exploratory Search: Beyond the Query-Response Paradigm,” that, “In many ways, exploratory search is as much about the journey through the information space as the destination” (2009). I felt this emphasis on process and journey to be a valuable lens in which to evaluate Pinterest as a space that has qualities to support exploratory search, especially in the area of daily information seeking and leisure.

In this study, I was interested in the three following criteria: user interest, user engagement, and usefulness. From the time-on-task analysis and the participant’s level of experience, I was able to make some observations on user interest and user engagement that could be further explored with a larger sample size. Originally, I couldn’t decide on how valuable time would be to my study. Because I wanted to encourage browsing and exploratory search, I wondered whether I should even consider time as a measure because ideally, I would want the participant to spend a long time on each task. In the end though, time proved to be fruitful in revealing some difference between participants in my sample.

By evaluating the time-on-tasks and the participants’ experience level, I was able to make a few observations. Experienced users tended to spend more time on each task, while novice users spent less. However, I realized that this engagement could be positive or negative—either the participant really wanted to continue searching and adding ideas to their board, or they weren’t able to find what they were looking for. In general, experienced users spent more time on the tasks and used more features than novice users. But one out of the two novice users (Participant 5, who has been using Pinterest for less than 6 months), had more to say about categorization and how the search system could better support her.

Experienced users seemed to have more knowledge and a better idea of how they use Pinterest, as well as more expectations for the system and website. This made their opinion on best and worst Pinterest features more credible because of their experience and interaction with the website design and features. Experienced users also spent more time browsing, they used more search queries to engage in exploratory search, and they paid more attention to the creation and naming of boards.

In terms of usefulness, each participant found Pinterest to be useful, but surprisingly, they all used it in different ways. For example, one participant was very community-driven and so her interaction on Pinterest was more public and social—the first thing she would do on Pinterest is check her notifications and see if other users have repined her post. However, for the majority of the participants, they used Pinterest more privately—they didn’t follow other users and they created “secret” boards, that are only for their viewing. All participants used Pinterest as a way to gather and organize ideas and images, and predictably, they were all drawn to the visual aspects of the Pinterest search results and liked to spend time on the site itself and go through their feed (this is especially true for experienced Pinterest users who had personalized their homepage to receive results that met their interest and aesthetics).

The real difficulty with this project is the small sample size. Due to only six participants split between four experienced Pinterest users and two novice users, I don’t feel like it’s possible to make any conclusive statements about Pinterest and the differences observed here without further research. Fortunately, this is pilot study and from that lens, this project yielded many points of discovery for me as a researcher that could later be developed into a more nuanced project.

06

Surprises

I was surprised that two out of the six participants were unfamiliar with the Pinterest website because they always used Pinterest on their mobile devices. This then created a learning curve that needed to happen during the session so that the participants would be able to complete the three search tasks. Even though these two participants were both experienced users, having used Pinterest for over a year, they had trouble navigating the website and needed time to figure out where to create a board and how to follow external links. I hadn’t considered how different the interface of a website compared to a mobile app would be, which made me consider whether or not I should conduct a study that allowed experienced Pinterest user to use the device/interface that they are familiar with.

I was also surprised by how much the participants related Pinterest to other systems and platforms, predominantly Google as a companion search engine and Etsy, if the user was interested in shopping for images that are posted on Pinterest. This introduced the idea of how users will use multiple platforms and system in conjunction with each other as a way to support, supplement, and vary their search results. It reminded me that users may employ two systems in conjunction with each other to inform their search, build knowledge, solve problems, and as a way to cross-check information to ensure its credibility. For example, when discussing Task 1 (planning a trip to Denmark), all participants talked about using Pinterest along with other search engines and platforms to gather more information about where to go and what to do. In this particular scenario, participants explained that they would use Google to focus on information/text-intensive results and retrieve factual information such as transit systems and currency. Then, they would look up specific place names on Pinterest and see what else they could find and discover. Each participant seemed to have a clear idea of what they wanted to use Pinterest for.

07

Conclusion

At the end of my test sessions, I always wished that I had more time, especially because browsing allows for more engagement. I felt like some of the participants felt pressured about the time or wanted to look and browse more. Perhaps they would have done so if they were on their own and not a part of a study. I also wanted to interview one or two more novice Pinterest users because the two novice users compared to the four experienced users did not feel balanced to me. If I could do my recruiting again, I would want to pay more attention to this level in experience as well as diversity in age. (Because I was recruiting from people that I knew, it turned out that all of them ended up in the 25-34 age range.) If I tried to recruit from a wider pool of Pinterest users, I wonder what the dominant age ranges for Pinterest users would be.

In terms of research design, I would replace Task 3 (finding a Mother’s Day card and following an account) with a different task or change it. It was immediately apparent the participants were not interested in using Pinterest to shop or follow another user’s account. I found it interesting that most of the participants preferred to create boards and save pins for their own inspiration and use, but didn’t really care to engage with the Pinterest community at large. They were more invested in creating their own aesthetic, creating mood/vision boards and saving ideas for future renovation/decoration plans. If I were to revise this task, I would have asked the participants to search card ideas that they would be interested in crafting themselves, as it would allow me to explore Pinterest as a space of inspiration.

Overall, I enjoyed this study. I learned about features that I didn’t know about at the start of this study and was surprise in each session by how differently each participant used Pinterest, even when there was overlap in the topics that they liked. My favorite surprise was learning about the participants attention to naming their boards. Particularly among the experienced participants, they revealed that naming a board is important because it reflects the mindset the participant is in when creating the board and also serves as a creative catalyst that will set the mood, aesthetic, and/or theme for the board. Despite knowing that the search task was just a scenario, two of the participants spent time carefully formulating a name for their board, which to me reflects the playfulness, seriousness, and care that users place into what they create and gather on Pinterest. All the participants seemed interested in using Pinterest as a way to define themselves and the world around them in a light-hearted and enjoyable way.
